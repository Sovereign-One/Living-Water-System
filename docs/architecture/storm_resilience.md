# Storm Resilience Architecture

## Hydraulic Air Compression + Pneumatic Floodwater Management

**Status:** Conceptual engineering architecture only  
**Origin:** Generalized from prior site-specific HBA-01 conceptual screening work, 18 October 2026  
**Not for construction**

## Purpose

This document generalizes the HBA-01 storm-resilience concept into a place-agnostic architecture that can be evaluated for flood-prone communities, drainage systems, underground storage networks, tunnels, channels, reservoirs, and other stormwater infrastructure.

The concept is intended as a complementary resilience layer. It does not assume that existing pumps, reservoirs, tunnels, channels, gates, levees, drainage systems, or power supplies should be removed.

The central question is whether part of the hydraulic energy already present during a storm can be redirected into useful flood-management work.

## Storm Judo Principle

The design philosophy is simple:

**Do not assume that the only way to survive a storm is to overpower its water. Where usable hydraulic head exists, redirect a portion of the storm's own energy into compression, storage, pumping, and controlled conveyance while preserving safe bypass and graceful failure.**

The objective is not to defeat water everywhere.

The objective is to make the system increasingly capable of using, redirecting, storing, passing, or safely yielding to water when storm conditions exceed normal operating assumptions.

## Core Energy Path

A generalized HBA-01 energy pathway is:

Stormwater / floodwater hydraulic head  
↓  
Controlled intake  
↓  
Air entrainment / hydraulic air compressor  
↓  
Downcomer  
↓  
Air-water separation  
↓  
Compressed-air header and storage  
↓  
Pressure regulation and distribution  
↓  
Pneumatic displacement pumping or other useful pneumatic work

The hydraulic air compressor may use a trompe or Taylor-compressor principle.

The water used for compression is separated from the compressed air and returned through a controlled water-only conveyance path to an appropriate downstream or receiving location.

The compressed air can then be used to move floodwater from storage, low points, tunnels, basins, or other controlled sources toward a safe receiving location.

## Complementary Resilience Layer

The architecture is not proposed as a replacement for conventional flood infrastructure.

A real system may combine:

- conventional electric pumps;
- gravity conveyance;
- stormwater tunnels;
- regulating reservoirs;
- detention or retention storage;
- channels and river works;
- gates and control structures;
- emergency generators;
- hydraulic air compression;
- compressed-air storage;
- pneumatic displacement pumping;
- bypasses and relief paths.

The value of HBA-01 would be its ability to provide an additional energy-recovery and fail-soft pathway.

Potentially useful operating conditions include:

- grid loss;
- constrained electrical supply;
- prolonged storm operation;
- severe exceedance events;
- loss of individual pump stations;
- conditions where stored pneumatic energy can support limited continued pumping.

The concept should not be assumed to provide complete flood protection or unlimited pumping capacity.

## Fail-Soft Architecture

Storm-resilience infrastructure should not depend on perfect operation.

A responsible implementation would require:

- conventional gates;
- hydraulic bypasses;
- overflow paths;
- pressure relief;
- debris control;
- sediment management;
- monitoring;
- isolation;
- emergency shutdown;
- safe downstream return of compression water;
- operating rules that do not create unacceptable upstream stage increases.

A failure of the hydraulic air-compression system should not convert the resilience feature into a new flood hazard.

Where practical, the system should degrade toward safe hydraulic passage rather than toward blockage, uncontrolled pressure, or upstream storage.

## Dirty-Water Reality

Stormwater is not clean process water.

Any hydraulic air-compression system intended for flood service must be evaluated under realistic storm conditions that may include:

- floating debris;
- sediment;
- trash;
- vegetation;
- entrained air;
- rapidly changing flow;
- rapidly changing head;
- variable water quality;
- abrasive material;
- biological material.

Potential protective features may include:

- coarse debris racks;
- sacrificial or cleanable forebays;
- sediment bypasses;
- accessible screens;
- flushing paths;
- staged intake structures;
- maintainable separators;
- components that can shed stages or bypass flow when partially obstructed.

The architecture should fail gracefully when debris or sediment exceeds the capacity of individual components.

## Sacrificial Debris and Energy-Dissipation Forebay

Before stormwater reaches a hydraulic air compressor, a large upstream forebay may be used as a **sacrificial conditioning zone**.

Its purpose is not fine treatment.

Its purpose is to intercept the destructive portion of the storm load before that load reaches sensitive downstream infrastructure.

Potential functions include:

- capture of boulders, logs, vehicles, trash, and other large debris;
- spreading concentrated flood flow across a wider cross section;
- deliberate momentum reduction;
- turbulence and controlled energy dissipation;
- upward deflection of portions of the flow;
- temporary dispersion of water into spray, droplets, and aerated flow;
- sediment deposition in zones designed for later removal;
- protection of downstream hydraulic-air-compression intakes.

Airborne spray or aerosol does not eliminate stormwater volume. Water lofted into the air will generally return to the drainage system or surrounding surface. However, temporary airborne dispersion, droplet breakup, air drag, turbulence, and repeated impact can increase residence time and dissipate part of the incoming kinetic energy. In a resilience system, even short delays or reductions in concentrated momentum may have value if they increase warning, evacuation, or downstream response time.

### Directionally Biased Flow Geometry

The forebay may include massive fins, baffles, ramps, deflectors, or channel geometry arranged to resist destructive inflow while still allowing controlled downstream passage.

One conceptual inspiration is fluidic rectification or Tesla-valve-like geometry at infrastructure scale.

This is an analogy, not a claim that a full-scale debris-laden flood will reproduce laboratory Tesla-valve behavior.

The engineering objective is to investigate whether geometry can create preferential resistance, repeated turning, recirculation, upward deflection, and path-length increase for incoming floodwater without producing unacceptable upstream stage or redirecting hazard toward people or critical assets.

The top surfaces of selected baffles may be shaped as upward ramps so that portions of high-velocity flow are deliberately projected upward into a controlled sacrificial volume.

In plain terms, the system may intentionally make part of the floodwater spend time moving **up, apart, and through air** instead of continuing horizontally as one dense destructive mass.

Any such geometry must be evaluated for:

- secondary scour;
- rebound and redirected jets;
- splash and spray zones;
- erosion;
- structural impact;
- debris accumulation;
- upstream backwater;
- bypass behavior;
- public access and exclusion;
- downstream hydraulic effects.

Energy dissipation is useful only when the resulting forces and flow paths are directed into locations designed to receive them.

### Debris Capture as a Deliberate Sacrifice

Large debris racks or impact structures may intentionally retain material that would otherwise continue downstream.

Clogging is therefore not automatically a failure.

If a structure safely captures a boulder, tree, vehicle, or debris mass that would otherwise strike people, buildings, bridges, tunnels, or flood-control machinery, the retained debris may represent successful hazard interception.

The design problem becomes managing the hydraulic consequences of that interception.

The forebay should therefore provide:

- large debris-storage volume;
- redundant flow paths;
- overflow or bypass capacity;
- access for inspection;
- stable working surfaces;
- sediment and debris staging areas;
- means to isolate damaged sections;
- clear post-event cleanup routes.

### Post-Storm Heavy-Equipment Access

Maintainability should be designed into the forebay from the beginning.

The facility should be laid out so that post-event cleanup can be performed by heavy earthmoving equipment rather than by improvised manual access.

Depending on site scale, access may be designed for equipment in the range of:

- D9-class crawler dozers;
- large off-road articulated haul trucks;
- 550-class hydraulic excavators;
- loaders, cranes, and support equipment appropriate to the debris load.

This may require:

- all-weather access roads;
- generous turning radii;
- equipment ramps;
- stabilized working pads;
- redundant ingress and egress;
- bridge and culvert capacities appropriate to cleanup equipment;
- protected fuel and staging areas;
- safe access after partial system damage;
- debris stockpile and haul-out zones.

The forebay should be treated as an infrastructure asset that is expected to become dirty, damaged, and obstructed during successful operation.

Its post-event recovery plan is therefore part of the design, not an afterthought.

### Life-Safety Function

The forebay does not need to eliminate a flood to be valuable.

If it reduces concentrated debris momentum, slows the arrival of the destructive front, or creates additional warning and evacuation time, that may be a meaningful resilience benefit even if downstream property damage still occurs.

For extreme events, the design priority may explicitly be:

**protect life first, preserve critical evacuation and response time second, protect property where practicable third.**

This sacrificial forebay is upstream of the hydraulic air compressor.

Its role is to make the water delivered to the compressor intake more manageable by removing large debris and reducing destructive flow concentration before energy recovery begins.

## Hydraulic Conditions Required

The concept depends on recoverable hydraulic head.

For any candidate location, engineering evaluation should establish:

- available flow;
- available gross head;
- realistic conveyance losses;
- acceptable upstream and downstream water-surface effects;
- air-entrainment performance;
- separator pressure;
- hydraulic stability;
- transient behavior;
- downstream return capacity.

No universal head, flow, or pressure requirement is established by this document.

The appropriate configuration is site-specific.

## Pneumatic Floodwater Management

Compressed air produced during storm flow may be stored and used to operate pneumatic displacement pumps.

A generalized pumping path is:

Compressed-air storage  
↓  
Regulation / controls  
↓  
Pneumatic displacement chamber  
↓  
Floodwater displacement  
↓  
Controlled discharge to a safe receiving location

The Living Water System's **Desert Whale** concept is one candidate pneumatic displacement mechanism.

The architecture does not require the Desert Whale specifically. Other suitable pneumatic displacement technologies may be substituted and independently evaluated.

## Relationship to the Compressed-Air Utility

Storm-resilience operation can connect to the broader compressed-air utility described elsewhere in this repository.

During a major storm, the priority may shift toward flood-management loads.

Possible uses of stored compressed air may include:

- pneumatic floodwater pumping;
- gate or valve actuation;
- controls;
- emergency mechanical work;
- other resilience-critical pneumatic loads.

Any shared compressed-air network would require operating rules that protect emergency reserve capacity during a storm.

## Storage as Time Separation

One potential advantage of compressed air is the ability to separate the timing of energy capture from the timing of pumping demand.

Stormwater may produce usable hydraulic head at one location while floodwater removal is needed at another location or at a different point in the event.

Storage may therefore allow:

Hydraulic energy available now  
↓  
Compressed-air production  
↓  
Temporary pneumatic storage  
↓  
Useful floodwater pumping when and where required

The practical value of this time separation depends on storage volume, leakage, pressure, piping losses, controls, safety, and required pumping duty.

## Long-Horizon Resilience Principle

The mechanical system is only one layer.

A second and independent planning principle is to reduce the consequence of flooding over infrastructure life cycles.

Where technically, socially, and economically practicable:

- avoid adding new critical vulnerability in flood-prone corridors;
- relocate critical infrastructure as renewal opportunities arise;
- redesign assets so flooding produces recoverable rather than catastrophic failure;
- preserve or create floodable open space where appropriate;
- allow selected parks, roads, landscapes, or other non-critical areas to temporarily accept water where safely designed to do so;
- maintain evacuation, emergency access, and recovery pathways;
- design for conditions beyond the nominal event where consequence warrants it.

The goal is not to promise that water will never exceed a design boundary.

The goal is to reduce the consequences when it does.

This long-horizon planning principle is separate from the hydraulic performance of HBA-01 and should be evaluated independently.

## Energy Accounting

The architecture does not create energy.

It attempts to recover a portion of hydraulic potential energy already present in moving or falling stormwater and convert some of that energy into compressed air and useful work.

Any evaluation must account for losses through:

- conveyance;
- air entrainment;
- hydraulic air compression;
- air-water separation;
- pneumatic storage;
- compressed-air transmission;
- leakage;
- regulation;
- pneumatic-to-water conversion;
- discharge pumping.

Round-trip performance must be measured rather than assumed.

## What Would Falsify or Stop the Concept?

The concept should be rejected, modified, or limited if serious engineering evaluation shows that:

- insufficient recoverable head remains after realistic losses;
- the system creates unacceptable upstream stage effects;
- air-entrainment or compression performance is too low or unstable;
- debris or sediment loads make reliable operation impractical;
- pneumatic storage, piping, controls, or pumping provide insufficient useful performance;
- cavitation, surge, air carryover, structural, geotechnical, seismic, or constructability constraints are unacceptable;
- integration with existing stormwater infrastructure creates operational conflicts or unsafe failure modes;
- maintenance burden is unreasonable;
- a conventional alternative provides materially better lifecycle resilience, reliability, safety, and cost for the same function.

The objective is to protect the investigation, not the mechanism.

## Minimum Responsible Test Sequence

A generalized test sequence is:

1. Perform independent hydraulic routing and modeling using site-controlled geometry, structures, inflow hydrographs, roughness, and downstream boundary conditions.
2. Test hydraulic air compression at bench scale using realistic dirty water, debris and sediment controls, air fraction, pressure, and transient conditions.
3. Independently test the pneumatic displacement pump at representative lifts, pressures, and duty cycles.
4. Test an integrated small-scale loop including bypass, pressure relief, controls, separation, storage, and energy accounting.
5. Evaluate failure states and exceedance behavior.
6. Only if the preceding gates are passed, conduct a site-specific pilot feasibility study.

## Evidence, Assumptions, and Unknowns

For each application, the project should distinguish:

### Established site information

Examples include surveyed geometry, drainage-system configuration, existing storage, existing pumps, published hydrology, and verified operational constraints.

### Conceptual geometry

Proposed shafts, tunnels, downcomers, separators, air-storage locations, return paths, and pumping locations remain conceptual until site-specific design is completed.

### Assumed efficiencies

Conveyance, hydraulic-air-compressor, storage, transmission, and pneumatic-pump efficiencies must be explicitly identified as assumptions until measured.

### Calculated outputs

Power, energy, pressure, storage, pumping volume, velocity, and head-loss calculations are only as reliable as their inputs and assumptions.

### Not established

Unless demonstrated by a project-specific study, this architecture does not establish:

- integrated prototype performance;
- calibrated hydraulic performance;
- structural design;
- geotechnical feasibility;
- seismic performance;
- lifecycle cost;
- permitting feasibility;
- environmental acceptance;
- construction readiness.

## Modular Development

The storm-resilience architecture is intentionally modular.

Failure of one component does not automatically invalidate every other component.

For example:

- pneumatic pumping does not require abandonment of electric pumps;
- hydraulic air compression does not require every stormwater facility to use it;
- long-horizon floodplain-risk reduction does not depend on successful pneumatic energy recovery;
- compressed-air storage can be evaluated independently from a specific displacement-pump geometry;
- conventional infrastructure remains available in parallel.

Each mechanism should survive or fail on its own engineering merit.

## Origin and Generalization

This architecture was generalized from an earlier **site-specific HBA-01 conceptual screening package**, prepared by Malak Bahrami, PE, dated 18 October 2026.

The earlier package evaluated a specific conceptual alignment and event screening. Those site-specific dimensions, flows, heads, capacities, event calculations, and facility references are intentionally not carried into this generalized architecture.

- explicit falsification criteria;
- staged testing;
- long-horizon reduction of flood consequence.

## Development Status

This document is an open engineering architecture.

It is not a sealed engineering document, construction specification, calibrated hydraulic model, or claim that the system has been validated.

It is an invitation to test the physical concept.

If the concept fails, discard it.

If a portion survives serious engineering review, improve it.

The objective is useful and resilient infrastructure.
