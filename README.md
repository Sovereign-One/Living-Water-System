# Living Water System

Open-source conceptual engineering framework exploring the use of wave motion, water, air, gravity, biomimetic materials, and passive environmental energy for water lifting, energy storage, conveyance, and desalination.

## Overview

The **Living Water System** is an evolving open-source engineering research concept.

Its central question is whether naturally available environmental energy can be deliberately arranged into useful water and energy infrastructure.

The system explores several interacting processes:

- ocean-wave-driven pressure oscillation;
- incremental elevation of seawater;
- storage of gravitational potential energy;
- hydraulic air compression using a trompe;
- compressed-air-powered water movement and other pneumatic uses;
- resilient water conveyance;
- passive or low-energy desalination;
- modular, maintainable infrastructure.

Natural phenomena are used as sources of engineering inspiration, not as proof that the proposed system will work as intended.

The concepts remain subject to hydraulic analysis, energy accounting, materials testing, prototyping, environmental review, lifecycle evaluation, and independent criticism.

## System Architecture

### Stage 1 — Water Elevator

**Principle:** Ocean-wave motion is used to create alternating pressure conditions.

The original water-elevator concept uses pressure oscillation generated by wave motion to incrementally move seawater upward.

A later refinement arranges the repeated lifting elements in a **helical configuration** around a tower.

The pressure plume or oscillation chamber is conceptually separate from the water being elevated. A separate seawater intake supplies the water moving through the elevator.

Each repeated cup-and-pipe element is intended to provide a relatively small incremental lift. The system therefore behaves conceptually as a hydraulic ratchet rather than attempting to lift the entire water column in one event.

The cup and pipe geometry is also intended to establish a stable hydrostatic resting condition between pressure cycles so that one-way valves are used primarily to control transient flow direction rather than continuously carrying the full static water column.

These operating hypotheses require hydraulic modeling and physical testing.

### Stage 2 — Trompe Protocol

**Principle:** Falling water can entrain air and compress it under hydrostatic pressure.

Water elevated during Stage 1 can descend through a trompe-type system.

During descent:

1. flowing water entrains atmospheric air;
2. the air-water mixture travels downward;
3. hydrostatic pressure compresses the entrained air;
4. compressed air separates from the water and is collected.

The resulting compressed air represents an intermediate energy-storage and distribution medium.

Potential applications include:

- water pumping;
- operation of the Desert Whale pump;
- pneumatic tools;
- air motors;
- mechanical drives;
- electrical generation through appropriate machinery;
- controls and actuators;
- community-scale compressed-air distribution.

A distributed compressed-air network is a conceptual infrastructure extension of the system, not a validated design. Moisture, transmission losses, leakage, pipe sizing, pressure regulation, storage efficiency, safety, corrosion, and end-use efficiency remain engineering questions.

Historical and technical inspiration includes traditional trompes and later hydraulic air-compression systems.

### Stage 3 — Desert Whale Pump Station

The **Desert Whale** is a conceptual air-displacement water pump intended to use compressed air generated upstream.

The concept uses a cyclic pump chamber in which:

1. water enters the chamber;
2. the water level raises a float-operated mechanism;
3. the inlet closes;
4. compressed air pressurizes the chamber;
5. water is displaced into the downstream conveyance system;
6. chamber pressure is relieved;
7. the inlet reopens and the cycle repeats.

The design is intended to convert stored pneumatic energy into intermittent water movement while minimizing reliance on conventional electrically driven pumps.

Valve sequencing, control reliability, pressure requirements, efficiency, maintenance, and failure behavior require further development.

### Stage 4 — Gastropod Composite Pipe

The **Gastropod Pipe** is a biomimetic composite-pipe concept inspired by naturally occurring layered protective structures.

The proposed configuration combines:

- a vitrified clay or ceramic-lined internal core; and
- a ductile-iron external structural shell.

The concept is intended to explore whether the corrosion resistance of ceramic materials and the structural capacity of ductile iron can be combined into a durable pressure conduit.

The proposed manufacturing sequence remains an unresolved engineering problem.

Important development questions include:

- thermal compatibility between the ceramic core and molten iron;
- thermal shock;
- differential thermal expansion;
- bonding and interface behavior;
- residual stresses;
- fabrication tolerances;
- corrosion performance;
- pressure capacity;
- inspection and repair.

No claim is made that the proposed fabrication process has been validated.

## Hibah Rooftop Desalination Concept

The **Hibah** concept explores distributed rooftop desalination using solar energy and gravity.

The current concept includes:

- controlled brine inflow;
- hydraulic level spreading;
- shallow sheet flow;
- coconut-coir or other replaceable media;
- solar evaporation;
- condensation and freshwater recovery;
- residual-brine management;
- potential recovery or reuse of spent materials.

Hibah is currently treated as a downstream or distributed subsystem rather than a mandatory numbered stage in every Living Water System configuration.

Its evaporation rate, condensation efficiency, water quality, salt accumulation, media performance, maintenance requirements, environmental impacts, and lifecycle economics require further investigation.

## Water and Energy Networks

The broader Living Water System can be viewed as two interacting infrastructure networks.

### Water Network

Source water → water elevator → elevated storage → hydraulic energy conversion → pumping → conveyance → desalination or other treatment → beneficial use

### Energy Network

Wave motion → gravitational potential energy → falling water → trompe → compressed air → storage/distribution → pumping, pneumatic work, mechanical work, or electricity generation

The purpose of this architecture is not to imply lossless energy conversion.

Each transition introduces losses that must be measured and accounted for.

## Maintainability and Environmental Considerations

Long-term operation would require deliberate consideration of:

- biofouling;
- sediment and particulate accumulation;
- exclusion of aquatic organisms from the water intake;
- intake screening or filtration;
- cup and pipe flushing;
- cleanouts and inspection access;
- replaceable components;
- isolation and bypass capability;
- corrosion;
- marine growth;
- compressed-air moisture;
- residual-brine management;
- material recovery and end-of-life reuse.

One filtration concept under consideration is replaceable porous ceramic media that could potentially be recovered, crushed, repurposed, or recycled after service.

This remains a development concept rather than a specified treatment process.

## Development Philosophy

Known problems are not hidden from the project.

An unresolved engineering issue is part of the design record until evidence resolves it.

The project therefore attempts to distinguish among:

- proposed mechanisms;
- working hypotheses;
- known uncertainties;
- engineering calculations;
- observations;
- validated results;
- failed concepts.

A concept may ultimately be improved, substantially changed, or rejected.

The objective is to protect the investigation rather than protect any individual design.

## Open Engineering and Known Issues

The Living Water System is intentionally published before every engineering question has been resolved.

Current development questions include hydraulic calibration, energy efficiency, biofouling, filtration, organism exclusion, component cleaning, material fabrication, compressed-air moisture, pneumatic transmission losses, corrosion, desalination performance, maintainability, scalability, and lifecycle performance.

These questions are intended to become a structured engineering backlog using repository documentation and GitHub Issues as the project develops.

Contributions that identify failure mechanisms, overlooked losses, environmental impacts, better materials, improved configurations, test methods, or alternative interpretations are valuable to the project.

## Intent

This work is offered as an **idea gift** and an open platform for exploration.

It is not presented as a finished commercial product.

The goal is to make the concepts sufficiently clear and accessible that others can examine them, challenge them, model them, test them, improve them, adapt them, or reject them on engineering grounds.

## Disclaimer

These materials are shared for research, discussion, education, and inspiration.

They are not construction documents, certified engineering specifications, or approval for real-world implementation.

Real-world application requires appropriate engineering analysis, environmental evaluation, permitting, safety review, testing, and qualified professional involvement.
